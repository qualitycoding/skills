# Sources for proper-english

Each entry gives the publisher, the claim it supports, and the rule in `SKILL.md` that uses it. URLs were read through web search results. Claims are paraphrased, not quoted at length. Where a source is a secondary summary of a primary authority, the primary authority is named.

## Correlative conjunctions

1. University of Texas School of Law, "Both parallel and pleasing" (legal writing blog). Lists both … and, either … or, neither … nor, not only … but also; the part of speech after the first conjunction must also follow the second; faulty example where "neither" precedes a verb and "nor" a preposition. https://sites.utexas.edu/legalwriting/2020/03/30/both-parallel-and-pleasing/ (Rules 1.1, 1.4)
2. PrepEdu, "Correlative Conjunctions: Definition, Full List, Rules". Pairs listed; parallel structure; proximity rule for either/or and neither/nor. https://prepedu.com/en/blog/correlative-conjunctions-in-english (Rules 1, 2)
3. Merriam-Webster, entry "nor". Defines "nor" as introducing the second or last member of a series in which each item is negated, and as introducing and negating a following clause or phrase; links a usage guide on "neither nor or neither or". https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/nor- (Rules 1.1, 1.2)
4. Columbia Journalism Review, "Neither regions" (Language Corner). Reports Merriam-Webster: "neither … or" is neither archaic nor wrong, though "neither" is usually followed by "nor"; American Heritage lists "nor" as the usual usage; after "not" rather than "neither", nearly everyone agrees on "or": "I have not peanut butter or jelly." https://www.cjr.org/language_corner/neither_regions.php (Rules 1.1, 1.2, choosing section)
5. MasterClass, "Correlative conjunctions explained". Negative verb phrase plus "neither … nor" is a double negative ("I can't neither see it nor hear it" is wrong; "I can neither see it nor hear it" is right); no comma splitting the pair when both halves share the verb. https://www.masterclass.com/articles/correlative-conjunctions-explained (Rules 1.3, 1.5)
6. Minnesota State University Moorhead, TESL 654 course page "Compounding: Correlative Conjunctions". Parallel structure applies to all correlative structures; joining like with like. Example: "wanted neither to sing nor to dance" as the parallel form. https://web.mnstate.edu/houtsli/tesl654/complex/compounding6.htm (Rule 1.4)
7. Dictionary.com, entry "neither". Some usage guides limit "neither" to two items, but it often introduces a series of three or more: "neither skillful nor well-prepared nor honest". https://app.dictionary.com/browse/neither (Rule 1.6)

## Subject-verb agreement

8. University of Wisconsin-Madison Writing Center, "Subject-verb Agreement". Singular subjects joined by or, nor, either … or, neither … nor take a singular verb; with one singular and one plural subject the verb agrees with the nearer one; examples with television and radios. https://writing.wisc.edu/handbook/subjectverb/ (Rule 2)
9. Writing Commons, "What is subject-verb agreement?". Same proximity rule with instructor/students examples. https://writingcommons.org/?p=338 (Rule 2)
10. Columbia Journalism Review, "Either wins", quoting Purdue OWL: when "neither … nor" links a singular and a plural term, put the plural term second and use a plural verb. https://www.cjr.org/language_corner/either_wins.php (Rule 2; primary authority: Purdue OWL, "Making Subjects and Verbs Agree", https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/grammar/subject_verb_agreement.html)

## Modifiers

11. Translation Bureau of Canada, Language Portal "Writing Tips Plus: Dangling modifiers". Defines a dangling modifier as one with no right word to modify. https://www.noslangues-ourlanguages.gc.ca/writing-tips-plus/dangling-modifiers (Rule 3)
12. Macmillan Learning, Bedford Handbook ("Repair misplaced and dangling modifiers"). Modifiers should point clearly to the words they modify; keep related words together. https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/bh9e/bh9e_ch12_1.html (Rule 3)
13. Purdue OWL, "Dangling Modifiers" handout (copy hosted by Cuyahoga/CGC at https://www.cgc.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/dangling-modifiers.pdf). A participial phrase whose doer is not stated is a dangling modifier; revise by naming the doer, or by combining the phrase and the main clause into one. Original: http://owl.english.purdue.edu/handouts/grammar/g_dangmod.html (Rule 3)
14. Roger Williams University School of Law, "Misplaced modifiers and dangling modifiers". Treats limiters (just, almost, hardly, nearly, simply) as a category needing careful placement. https://devlaw.rwu.edu/sites/law/files/downloads/Academic%20Success/Misplaced%20Modifiers%20and%20Dangling%20Modifiers%201.pdf (Rule 3)

## Word pairs

15. IEEE ProComm, "Word choice" (communication resources for engineers). Fewer for a smaller number of countable things, less for a limited amount or collective; among for a member of a group, between when two are specified. https://procomm.ieee.org/communication-resources-for-engineers/grammar/word-choice/ (Rule 4)
16. David Fox, University of Saskatchewan, "Grammar, Style, and Usage" (citing Steven Pinker). Fewer for countables and less for quantities; "less" is idiomatic with measures such as years, speeds and weights ("less than 5 kilograms"). https://sites.usask.ca/ceblipblog/2016/04/05/grammar-style-and-usage/ and https://sites.usask.ca/ceblipblog/tag/style/ (Rule 4)
17. Just Publishing Advice, "Between vs. Among: It's Not Just About The Number"; Proofed, "Word Choice: Between vs. Among". Both describe "between = two, among = more than two" as a simplification: "between" suits distinct relationships among three or more; "among" suits an undifferentiated group. https://justpublishingadvice.com/between-and-among/ and https://getproofed.com.au/?p=23709 (Rule 4)

## Primary references to consult when a rule is disputed

- Merriam-Webster's Dictionary of English Usage (dangling participles, "neither … or", "between"/"among").
- Purdue Online Writing Lab, general writing grammar section.
- The Chicago Manual of Style, grammar and usage chapter.
- Fowler's Dictionary of Modern English Usage.

These were not fetched in full for this skill; consult them directly before adding a rule that rests only on a secondary source.

## Notes on disputed or simplified points

- "no X or Y" versus "neither X nor Y": both are grammatical (sources 3, 4). The skill treats "neither … nor" as an optional emphasis choice, and treats "no X nor Y" and "neither X or Y" as errors or, at best, disfavoured forms.
- "between" for exactly two: source 15 states the two-versus-more rule plainly, while source 17 calls it a simplification. The skill follows source 17 for the exception and keeps the simple rule as the default.
