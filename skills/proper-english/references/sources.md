# Sources for proper-english

British and academic authorities only. Each entry gives the work, what it supports, which rule uses it, and the **read status**:

- **read**: the text of the page or passage was retrieved and read when the entry was written.
- **excerpt**: only a search-result excerpt or a quotation in a secondary source was seen.
- **consult**: named as an authority to check; not read.

Claims are paraphrased. American dictionaries, manuals and university pages are deliberately not used.

## Dictionaries and grammars (Oxford and Cambridge)

1. *Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary* (Oxford University Press), entry "neither", with its grammar point on *neither / either*. Defines "neither" as "not one nor the other of two", which sets the neither … nor pairing. After "neither" or "either" use a singular verb ("Neither candidate was selected"). *Neither of / either of* takes a plural noun or pronoun, and the verb may be singular or plural, the plural being more informal. When "neither … nor" or "either … or" joins two singular nouns, the verb may be singular or plural, the plural being more informal. https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/neither (**read**). Rules 2.1, 2.3, 3.
2. Huddleston, R., Payne, J. and Peterson, P., 'Coordination and supplementation', in Huddleston, R. and Pullum, G. K. (eds), *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2002), pp. 1273–1362. As quoted in the Language Log post "Neither sentence nor sentence?" (https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=44935): "neither" can occur, like "either", in multiple as well as binary coordination; like "both" it cannot normally occur initially in a coordination of main clauses (starred example: *Neither did he oversleep nor was his bus late*); such structures are usually avoided in favour of coordination below clause level ("We are neither trying to keep out immigrants, nor favouring the well-to-do"). "Nor" is described as a coordinator joining negative alternatives, often with "neither". (**excerpt**, via a secondary source; consult the book). Rules 2.1, 2.3, 2.5, 2.6, 2.7.
3. Grammar Monster (Mark Leather, UK), "Either/or: plural or singular verb". Records the proximity rule (the nearest element governs the verb) beside the convention that a plural element requires a plural verb, and advises putting the plural element nearest the verb so both conventions are satisfied. https://www.grammar-monster.com/lessons/either_or_plural_or_singular_verb.htm (**read** as excerpt; a teaching site, not an academic work: treat as secondary). Rule 3.
4. Walter, Liz, "Less or fewer?", *About Words*, Cambridge Dictionary blog (Cambridge University Press), 28 May 2014. "Less" with uncountable nouns (less sugar, less fuel); "fewer" with countable nouns (fewer people, fewer errors); "fewer than" sounds formal so "less than" is usual before numbers; "less than" is correct with periods of time and sums of money treated as single units; the retailer sign "10 items or less" was changed to "up to 10 items" after Plain English Campaign advice. https://dictionaryblog.cambridge.org/2014/05/28/less-or-fewer/ (**read**). Rule 5.
5. Butterfield, Jeremy (ed.), *Fowler's Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, 4th edn (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2015), as reviewed by Michael Quinion, World Wide Words: the entry on "less versus fewer" remarks that the facts of language are more complicated than simple rules allow. https://worldwidewords.org/re-fow1.html (**read** the review only; the book itself is **consult**). Rule 5.
6. *Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary*, entry "dangling participle": the correct form of the example is "While I was walking home, my phone rang." https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/dangling-participle (**excerpt**). Rule 4.

## Style guides (spelling and punctuation)

7. University of Oxford, *Style Guide* (Public Affairs Directorate), quick reference A–Z, 2025 edition, and the full guide (Michaelmas term 2014; updated Hilary term 2016). Spelling: use -ise, -isation, -yse, -ysation, not -ize, -ization, -yze, -yzation. Quotation marks: single quotation marks for quotations, direct speech and titles, double within them. The guide states it is an in-house guide for University staff and does not compete with OUP's professional guides. https://www.ox.ac.uk/sites/files/oxford/Style%20Guide%20quick%20reference%20A-Z%20%282025%29.pdf (**excerpt**). Rules 1 (spelling, quotation marks).
8. Modern Humanities Research Association, *MHRA Style Guide*, quick guide: short quotations (up to forty words) in single quotation marks and run on; double quotation marks for a quotation within a quotation; longer quotations set as an indented paragraph without quotation marks. https://www.mhra.org.uk/style/quick.html (**read** as excerpt). Rule 1 (quotation marks).
9. "Oxford spelling": Oxford University Press uses -ize in its publications, including the *Oxford English Dictionary*, and keeps -yse; the *OED* lists -ise as a frequent spelling of -ize; UK newspapers normally use -ise. Source is an encyclopaedia summary of OUP practice (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_spelling) (**excerpt**, tertiary; check against the *OED* and *New Hart's Rules*). Rule 1 (spelling).
10. Salt Publishing (UK), *Style guide for authors*: words always spelt -yse (analyse, paralyse, catalyse); a few words always -ize because no -ise alternative exists (capsize, seize, prize meaning to value); single quotation marks for speech and quoted matter. https://www.saltpublishing.com/pages/style-guide-for-authors (**excerpt**). Rule 1 (spelling, quotation marks).
11. MDPI, "Guide to British and American English" (publisher blog, 25 April 2024): in British English, single quotation marks are favoured, with double inside; punctuation follows the closing quotation mark when it belongs to a larger sentence, but not when the quotation is the whole sentence. https://blog.mdpi.com/2024/04/25/british-and-american-english/ (**excerpt**, secondary). Rule 1 (punctuation placement).
12. LanguageTool, "Why -ise / -ize" (British versus American spelling): -ce in defence and offence against American -se. https://languagetool.org/insights/post/ise-ize (**excerpt**, secondary, software publisher). Rule 1 (defence, offence).

## Rules held without a retrieved British authority

These rules appear in the skill marked **[unverified]**. They reflect accepted practice, but no British academic text was read for them. Verify each against the works in the "Consult" list before relying on it in a dispute.

- "Or", not "nor", after "no", "not", "never" and "without" (section 2, rule 2).
- No stacked negative with "neither" ("can't neither") (section 2, rule 8).
- Intervening phrases do not change a subject's number (section 3).
- Placement of limiters such as "only" and "just" (section 4).
- Between versus among (section 6).
- British spellings -our, -re, licence/license, practice/practise, grey, cheque, catalogue, programme/program (section 1).
- Date order and serial comma (section 1).

## Notes on disputed points

- **-ise or -ize.** Oxford University Press uses -ize (with -yse) as house spelling; the University of Oxford's own guide and UK newspapers use -ise (sources 7, 9). Both are British. The skill defaults to -ise and switches on request.
- **Singular or plural verb after "neither … nor" with two singular subjects.** The *OALD* records both, with the plural as the more informal (source 1). The skill uses the singular in formal prose.
- **"No X or Y" versus "neither X nor Y".** Both are grammatical for two negated items; the skill treats "neither … nor" as an optional emphasis, and does not call "no X or Y" an error.
